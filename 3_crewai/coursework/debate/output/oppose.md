I oppose the motion because **strict laws are the wrong tool for regulating LLMs**. They would likely do more harm than good.

First, **LLMs are a fast-moving general-purpose technology**, not a single industry with one clear risk profile. If you impose rigid laws now, you lock in today’s assumptions and slow down a field that is still evolving. By the time legislation is passed, the technology will have changed. That means strict rules will either be outdated or so broad that they become a blunt instrument.

Second, **strict laws protect incumbents more than the public**. Large companies can afford compliance teams, audits, and legal review; startups, researchers, and smaller competitors often cannot. The result is less competition, fewer new entrants, and more power concentrated in the very firms people are worried about. Overregulation does not democratize AI — it can entrench it.

Third, **the best way to reduce harm is targeted, flexible governance**, not sweeping legal restriction. We already have laws against fraud, defamation, privacy invasion, discrimination, and consumer deception. Enforce those laws against bad actors using LLMs. Add sector-specific standards where needed, like in healthcare or finance. That is smarter than creating rigid AI-specific laws that may accidentally suppress beneficial uses in education, accessibility, research, and productivity.

Finally, **strict laws create a false sense of security**. Harmful outputs will not disappear because Parliament or Congress passes a statute. Real safety comes from testing, transparency, technical safeguards, and rapid adaptation — not from static legal control.

So the issue is not whether LLMs should be left unchecked; they should not. The issue is whether we need **strict laws**. We don’t. We need **smart, flexible, and proportionate oversight** that reduces harm without strangling innovation.
